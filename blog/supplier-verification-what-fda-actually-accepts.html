Supplier verification: what FDA actually accepts

Four activities, one of which becomes mandatory and annual for serious hazards, plus the written determination that lets you use something else instead.

JC
James Chang Written with AI

"Supplier verification" sounds open-ended. It isn't: the regulation names the acceptable activities, and for one category of hazard, it specifies which one you must use and how often.

Checked against 21 CFR §1.506 on 6 September 2026. Start with FSVP for the first-time importer if you are new to this.

The four activities

§1.506 lists what counts:

  1. Onsite audits of the foreign supplier
  2. Sampling and testing of the food
  3. Review of the foreign supplier's relevant food safety records
  4. Other appropriate supplier verification activities

You choose based on the hazard, the food, the supplier's performance, and the nature of the control, and you write down why. The choice belongs in the record, not in your head.

The exception that removes the choice

Where a hazard has a reasonable probability that exposure will result in serious adverse health consequences or death — SAHCODHA in FDA's shorthand — and the hazard is controlled by the foreign supplier, §1.506(d)(2) requires:

an onsite audit of the foreign supplier before initially importing the food and at least annually thereafter

That is the default, and it's expensive — which is exactly why the alternative matters.

The written determination

You may use other activities, or audit less often, if you make an adequate written determination that they provide adequate assurance the hazard is controlled. The operative word is written: an unwritten judgment that an audit wasn't necessary isn't an alternative to an audit. It's a gap.

Two things can also substitute for the onsite audit, where conducted within one year:

  • inspection by FDA, another federal agency, or a state or local agency; or
  • inspection by the food safety authority of a country whose system FDA has recognised as comparable or determined to be equivalent.

It's worth checking before commissioning an audit: your supplier may already have been inspected in a way that counts.

Who may conduct the audit

The audit must be performed by a qualified auditor, and the regulation is explicit that you may not rely on the foreign supplier itself, or its employees, to conduct it — except for sampling and testing activities.

That rules out the most common shortcut: accepting the factory's own audit report as your verification. Their report can inform your evaluation, but it isn't your audit.

Approval comes before verification, and expires

§1.505 requires evaluation and approval of the supplier before importing, based on the hazard analysis and on the supplier's performance — its procedures, processes and practices, and its compliance history.

And it isn't permanent: reevaluation is required whenever new information surfaces about the hazards or the supplier's performance, and at least every 3 years regardless.

Three years is long enough that nobody remembers it's due, which is the entire problem. It belongs on a calendar attached to the supplier record, not in a vague annual intention.

A workable pattern

  • Classify the hazard first. SAHCODHA or not decides whether you are in the annual audit regime or choosing freely.
  • Ask what inspections already exist. An FDA or recognised-authority inspection within the year can replace the audit you were about to buy.
  • Write the determination whenever you deviate, at the time, with the reasoning.
  • Diarise the 3-year reevaluation per supplier, and treat a change of process or a compliance event as an early trigger.
  • Keep the auditor's independence provable. Who performed it, and their qualification, is part of the record.
