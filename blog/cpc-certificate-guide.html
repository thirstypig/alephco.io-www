Children's Product Certificate (CPC): The Complete Importer Guide for 2026

Everything you need to know about issuing a complete CPC — the 7 required elements, which CPSC testing standards apply, how to find an accredited lab, and how to keep the records organized.

You've sourced the perfect children's toy from your factory in Shenzhen. Samples look great; pricing works; your buyer at Target is excited. You're feeling good about this one. Then someone on your team asks: "Do we have the CPC for this?" And suddenly your Monday is gone.

If you've been there, you're not alone. The Children's Product Certificate is one of those compliance requirements that sounds straightforward — it's just a document, right? — but trips up importers constantly. Not because it's conceptually hard, but because the details matter, the regulation specifies content rather than a mandatory form, and getting it wrong can mean your shipment sits at the port while your retail buyer moves on to someone who has their paperwork together.

This guide walks through exactly what a CPC is, who needs one, what goes in it, which tests are required, and how to put one together without hiring an expensive compliance consultant.

What Is a Children's Product Certificate?

A Children's Product Certificate is a document required by the Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act (CPSIA) for children's products (subject to a children's product safety rule) manufactured in or imported into the United States. It certifies that the product has been tested by a CPSC-accepted third-party laboratory and complies with all applicable children's product safety rules.

Think of it as your product's passport. Imported children's products must be accompanied by one (16 CFR 1110.5), and CPSC can refuse admission to products without one. Retailers can refuse it. And the CPSC can pursue recalls, civil penalties, or both.

Here's the part that catches a lot of first-time importers off guard: you are responsible for issuing the CPC. Not your supplier. Not your factory. Not your testing lab. For imports, the importer of record — that's you — must issue the certificate, attest to it, and furnish it (16 CFR 1110.7). Your factory might hand you something they call a "CPC," but that doesn't replace yours: you remain legally responsible for the certificate's accuracy and completeness (16 CFR 1110.15).

Who Needs a CPC? (It's More Products Than You Think)

If your product is designed or intended primarily for children 12 years of age or younger, it's a "children's product" under CPSIA, and it needs a CPC. That definition is broader than most people expect.

The obvious categories are there — toys, car seats, cribs, pacifiers. But here's what catches people: children's clothing, children's jewelry, kids' backpacks, school supplies marketed to children, and children's furniture can all fall under the definition. (Cosmetics are not: they're regulated by FDA, not CPSC.) If the product's marketing, packaging, or design signals that a child is the primary user, that weighs heavily in the CPSC's analysis under 16 CFR 1200.2, where four factors are considered together.

A common mistake is assuming that because your product doesn't contain lead paint or small parts, you don't need a CPC. That's wrong. The CPC requirement is about the category of product, not the specific hazard. Even if your product passes every test with flying colors, you still need the certificate documenting that it was tested and compliant.

And if you're selling on Amazon, Walmart.com, or any major marketplace, expect to be asked for the CPC, and some platforms may ask you to upload the actual document. Check each marketplace's current requirements.

The 7 Required Elements of a Valid CPC

The CPSC specifies that every Children's Product Certificate must contain seven elements (16 CFR 1110.11). Miss one and the certificate doesn't meet the rule. Here they are:

  1. Product identification — At least one unique identifier (GTIN, model number, registered number, serial number, SKU, UPC, or an alternate identifier) plus a description specific enough to match the product to the certificate. "Children's toy" alone won't cut it.
  2. List of applicable CPSC rules — Every children's product safety rule, ban, standard, or regulation that applies to your product, each listed separately. We'll cover the common ones in the next section.
  3. Name and contact details of the certifying party — Name, street address, email, and phone. That's the US importer (you) for imports, or the domestic manufacturer (or private labeler). Again — not your overseas factory.
  4. Contact information for the person maintaining test records — Name, street address, email, and phone number of the individual (or a staffed position) who can produce the test reports if the CPSC comes asking.
  5. Date and place of manufacture — At least the month and year of manufacture, and the manufacturer's name, address, email, and phone.
  6. Date and place of testing — The most recent test date, and the name, address, email, and phone of each third-party lab the certificate relies on. For a CPC this must be a CPSC-accepted laboratory. Not any lab. Not an in-house lab. A CPSC-accepted one.
  7. The certifier's attestation — A statement that the product complies with the rules listed and the information is true and accurate. For eFiled certificates, the attestation is built into the filing.

One thing to note: the CPC must "accompany" the product or shipment. Since July 8, 2026 (January 8, 2027 for goods entered from a foreign trade zone), for imported products that means eFiling the certificate data with CBP at the time of entry (16 CFR 1110.13), either in full or as a reference to data you've entered in CPSC's Product Registry. You must also furnish the certificate to your distributors and retailers, and produce it within 24 hours if CPSC or CBP asks.

Which CPSC Testing Standards Apply to Your Product?

This is where things get specific. The "list of applicable rules" on your CPC depends on what you're importing. Here are the standards that come up most often:

  • CPSIA Section 101 — Lead in substrate — Total lead content in any accessible part may not exceed 100 ppm (15 U.S.C. 1278a). Applies to virtually every children's product.
  • CPSIA Section 101 — Lead in paint — Lead in paint and surface coatings may not exceed 90 ppm (0.009%) under 16 CFR 1303. If your product has paint, ink, or any coating, this applies.
  • 16 CFR 1501 — Small parts — Products for children under 3 can't have small parts that pose a choking hazard. The classic small parts cylinder test.
  • ASTM F963 — Toy safety — The big one for toys. Covers mechanical/physical hazards, flammability, chemical properties, electrical hazards, and more. If it's a toy, F963 almost certainly applies.
  • 16 CFR 1610 — Flammability of clothing textiles — If you're importing children's clothing, this applies. Sleepwear has even stricter standards under 16 CFR 1615/1616.
  • CPSIA Section 108 — Phthalates — Eight specified phthalates are prohibited above 0.1% in children's toys and child care articles (16 CFR 1307).
  • Tracking labels (CPSIA Section 103) — Not a testing standard per se, but your product and its packaging must have permanent, distinguishing marks (tracking labels), to the extent practicable, that identify the manufacturer or private labeler, the location and date of production, and the batch or run.

Depending on your product category, additional standards may apply. Children's furniture has its own set. Cribs, play yards, carriers, strollers — each has specific mandatory standards. The CPSC maintains a full list of regulated products on their site. It's not light reading, but it's the authoritative source.

How to Find a CPSC-Accepted Testing Lab

You can't use just any lab. Children's products must be tested by a laboratory that's been accepted by the CPSC for the specific tests you need. The CPSC maintains a list of accepted labs and the scope of each acceptance on its website (16 CFR 1112).

A few practical tips from importers who've been through this:

  • Use labs your factory already works with — Many factories in China, Vietnam, and other manufacturing hubs have relationships with large testing networks like SGS, Bureau Veritas, Intertek, and TUV — check the specific location on CPSC's list. Using a lab near your factory saves time and shipping costs on samples.
  • Confirm acceptance for your specific standards — A lab might be CPSC-accepted for ASTM F963 but not for 16 CFR 1610. Verify that the lab is accepted for every standard you need to certify against.
  • Get test reports in English — This sounds obvious, but if you're using a lab in Asia, make sure the reports are issued in English with the CPSC-accepted lab's name, address, and accreditation number clearly stated.
  • Budget time for testing — A full children's product test suite (lead, phthalates, F963, small parts) can take a few weeks; ask the lab for its current turnaround. Rush options exist but cost more.

One thing importers often overlook: you need to re-test and re-certify when you make a material change to the product — a change in design, manufacturing process, or component sourcing that could affect compliance (16 CFR 1107.23). New supplier for the fabric? Different paint? Those can be material changes. You'll need updated third-party test reports and a new CPC. Separately, periodic testing is required at least once a year unless you qualify for a longer interval (16 CFR 1107.21). This is where tracking becomes critical — and where spreadsheets start failing you.
