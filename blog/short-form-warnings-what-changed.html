Short-form Prop 65 warnings: what changed

The short warning now has to name a chemical. The deadline is January 2028, and it keys on when a product was manufactured and labelled, not when it sells.

JC
James Chang Written with AI

The short-form Prop 65 warning — the tiny one that says "Cancer — www.P65Warnings.ca.gov" and nothing else — has been amended, and the change removes the thing that made it attractive. It now has to name a chemical.

Checked against the regulation on 6 September 2026. Background: Prop 65 for importers.

The new short form

27 CCR §25603(b) requires the symbol, the word WARNING: in bold capitals, and one of these:

For carcinogens:

"Cancer risk from exposure to [name of chemical]. See www.P65Warnings.ca.gov."

or "Can expose you to [name of chemical], a carcinogen. See www.P65Warnings.ca.gov."

For reproductive toxicants:

"Risk of reproductive harm from exposure to [name of chemical]. See www.P65Warnings.ca.gov."

or "Can expose you to [name of chemical], a reproductive toxicant. See www.P65Warnings.ca.gov."

There are parallel forms for both endpoints together, and for a chemical listed under both. In every case, a chemical name is now mandatory.

The old form, and the actual deadline

§25603(c) preserves the old content, and this sentence is the one to read carefully:

A short-form warning on a product manufactured and labeled prior to January 1, 2028, may use the following content regardless of when the product is sold to a consumer.

Two things follow from that, and people routinely get both backwards:

  • The cutoff is manufacture and labelling, not sale. Stock made and labelled before 1 January 2028 can be sold afterwards, indefinitely. There is no requirement to relabel or withdraw existing inventory.
  • What changes on that date is what you may produce. Product manufactured and labelled from 1 January 2028 must carry the new form.

So "we have to relabel everything by 2028" is wrong, and expensive. The real question is when your labels and artwork change over, and whether anything you make after the cutoff still carries the old plate.

Why this is harder than it looks

Naming a chemical means knowing which chemical. The old short form let you warn without that — it was a hedge you could apply broadly when you suspected exposure but hadn't identified the source.

That hedge is gone. To use the new short form, you must be able to say which listed chemical, and where a warning covers both endpoints, §25601(b) requires a name for each — unless one chemical is listed for both and is identified as such.

That is a supply-chain data problem before it is a labelling problem, and it is the reason to start well before the artwork deadline.

The mechanics worth knowing

  • The short form is one of four permitted methods under §25602(a) — the others being a sign or shelf tag at the point of display, an electronic warning delivered before or during purchase without the buyer seeking it out, and the long-form label.
  • Type size follows §25601(c), and in no case smaller than 6-point type.
  • §25601(c) also requires that the warning be displayed "with such conspicuousness … as to render the warning likely to be seen, read, and understood by an ordinary individual under customary conditions of purchase or use". Small is allowed; hidden is not.

What to do now

  1. Find every SKU using a short-form warning. This is usually more than anyone expects, because it was the easy option.
  2. For each, identify the listed chemical driving the warning — per endpoint.
  3. Decide per SKU: switch to the long form, adopt the new short form, or establish you are below the level and remove the warning entirely.
  4. Set your artwork changeover so nothing is manufactured and labelled with the old content after 31 December 2027.
  5. Don't relabel existing stock. The sell-through is unlimited.

Step 2 is the long pole, and it is the one that depends on suppliers answering. Keeping the answer on the product record with its date is what makes step 4 a filter instead of a project.
