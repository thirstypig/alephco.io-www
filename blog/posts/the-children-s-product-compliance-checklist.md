Children's product obligations arrive as a bundle. Miss one and the others do not save you,
because they are checked by different people at different moments — a lab, a customs entry,
a recall, a marketplace audit.

This pulls the month's posts into one list. Checked on **6 September 2026**.

## 1. Decide whether it is a children's product — properly

[16 CFR §1200.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/part-1200): a consumer product
designed or intended primarily for children **12 years of age or younger**, judged on four
factors weighed together — your statement of intended use (if reasonable), how it is
packaged and advertised, how consumers commonly recognise it, and the CPSC's Age
Determination Guidelines.

⚠️ You do not get to settle this with a label. Factor 1 is qualified by "if such statement
is reasonable", and the four are weighed as a whole.

**If yes, everything below applies. If no, you likely still need a GCC.**

## 2. Test at a lab accepted for your specific rules

Not "a CPSC-accepted lab" — one whose
[§1112.13](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/part-1112) statement of scope covers the
rules your product is subject to. See
[choosing a lab](/blog/third-party-testing-choosing-a-cpsc-accepted.html).

## 3. Issue a Children's Product Certificate, as the importer

[16 CFR §1110.7](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/part-1110): for goods made outside
the US, **the importer is the finished product certifier**. Your supplier's certificate is
an input, not a substitute.

§1110.11 requires each applicable rule to be listed **separately**, plus the most recent
test date and place, the record-keeper's details and your attestation. See
[GCC vs CPC](/blog/gcc-vs-cpc-which-certificate-does-your.html).

## 4. eFile at entry

Since **8 July 2026**, certificate data elements must be filed electronically into CBP's
ACE at the time of entry (§1110.13). Foreign trade zone entries for consumption or
warehousing follow on **8 January 2027**.

⚠️ CPSC has said ACE will initially return **warnings** rather than rejections for missing
data. A shipment that cleared is not evidence your filing was right.

## 5. Put a tracking label on the product and the packaging

[15 U.S.C. 2063(a)(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/2063) requires permanent
marks enabling **two** determinations: yours (location, date, cohort, source) and the
purchaser's (manufacturer or private labeler, location, date, cohort). Most labels satisfy
one. See [tracking labels](/blog/tracking-labels-what-has-to-be-on.html).

## 6. Retest on schedule, and on material change

[§1107.21](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/part-1107): at least annually, or every
two years with a production testing plan, or every three with production testing at an
ISO/IEC 17025 lab. §1107.23: a material change in design, manufacturing or **component
sourcing** requires a new CPC with new testing.

Keep everything **five years** (§1107.26).

## The three that actually go wrong

In our experience it is rarely the initial certification that fails. It is:

- **A component swap nobody told you about.** It is a material change, it invalidates the
  certificate, and no system anywhere generates a notification. Ask "what changed since
  [date]?", not "has anything changed?"
- **A certificate that names no rules.** "Complies with all applicable CPSC regulations"
  does not meet §1110.11(a)(2), and it is the most common template error we see.
- **Records that live in an inbox.** Five years outlasts the supplier relationship, the
  laptop and often the employee.

None of these are hard problems. They are all the same problem: the obligation is
continuous and the paperwork was treated as an event.

Aleph keeps certificates, test dates and the rules they cite against each product, so
"which of these is out of date" is a filter rather than an archaeology project.
