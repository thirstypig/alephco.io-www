People describe the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act as a ban. It isn't one. It's a
presumption — and the distinction matters enormously for what you actually have to do
about it.

Checked against the statute, the Federal Register and CBP's own guidance on **10 September 2026**.

## The mechanism, precisely

CBP [enforces](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/forced-labor/UFLPA) "the rebuttable presumption
that goods mined, produced, or manufactured wholly or in part in the XUAR, or by an entity on
the UFLPA Entity List, are prohibited from U.S. importation under 19 U.S.C. § 1307."

The presumption is rebuttable, but the bar is high, and it has three parts. Under
[section 3(b) of the Act](https://www.congress.gov/117/plaws/publ78/PLAW-117publ78.htm), CBP
applies the presumption unless the importer of record has:

1. "fully complied with the guidance described in section 2(d)(6)" — the importer guidance
   in the government's UFLPA Strategy;
2. "completely and substantively responded to all inquiries for information" from CBP; and
3. shown, "by clear and convincing evidence, that the good, ware, article, or merchandise was
   not mined, produced, or manufactured wholly or in part by forced labor."

Clear and convincing evidence is, in
[CBP's own words](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/forced-labor/faqs-uflpa-enforcement), "a higher
standard of proof than a preponderance of the evidence, and generally means that a claim or
contention is highly probable." That's the part people underestimate: this isn't a checkbox
but a genuine evidentiary burden, and it sits entirely on the importer.

## What triggers a detention

CBP's
[operational guidance for importers](https://www.cbp.gov/sites/default/files/2026-06/fld_importer_guidance_final_06092026_0.pdf)
sorts shipments by whether they carry a "potential input" or a "direct input" from the XUAR
or a listed entity:

| What CBP has | What CBP can do |
|---|---|
| Information that a producer, supplier or vendor "may have a link" to the XUAR or a listed entity (a potential input) | Detain the shipment, or exclude it outright, "depending on the certainty of the connection" |
| Information that the good is made, wholly or in part, in the XUAR or by a listed entity (a direct input) | Exclude it: "the goods will be excluded" |

The link doesn't have to be your direct supplier. The guidance covers a good that "is
produced by or contains inputs from" the XUAR or a listed entity, which is why the Entity
List matters as much as it does.

## Who's actually on the list

The [UFLPA Entity List](https://www.dhs.gov/uflpa-entity-list) is published by DHS as chair
of the interagency Forced Labor Enforcement Task Force, and it's growing fast. The
[31 July 2026 expansion](https://www.dhs.gov/news/2026/07/31/dhs-announces-addition-43-companies-uflpa-entity-list)
— 43 companies in a single update — brought the list to **187 entities**, a 30% jump and, in
DHS's words, "the single largest-ever expansion of the List."

DHS highlighted aluminum, apparel, copper, cotton and tomato-product supply chains. The
[Federal Register notice](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/03/2026-15628/notice-regarding-the-uyghur-forced-labor-prevention-act-entity-list)
behind it goes further: it also lists companies for sourcing titanium and gold from the
region. And several of the new entities aren't in Xinjiang at all. The notice places them in
Henan, Jiangsu and Shandong, listed because they source materials such as cotton, aluminum,
titanium or gold *from* the XUAR. Being outside the region named in the law's title doesn't put a supplier
outside its reach.

## What it looks like from the importer's side

[PV Tech reported](https://www.pv-tech.org/vsun-solar-modules-detained-under-uflpa-could-knock-us30-million-off-company-earnings/)
in April 2026 that CBP had detained solar modules from **VSUN**, a Vietnam-based
manufacturer whose Japanese parent is Abalance. According to that report, Abalance said
"sufficient evidence to allow for an exception could not be confirmed," maintained that it
and VSUN "do not use raw materials originating from the Uyghur region," and projected a hit
of roughly ¥3–5 billion (about US$18–31 million) to annual earnings. VSUN said it was working
through US legal counsel to win release; failing that, it planned to sell the modules
outside the US at a lower price.

We couldn't find Abalance's own disclosure, so those figures are PV Tech's, not ours. The
mechanics don't depend on them, though. CBP's guidance is blunt that an importer who
challenges a detention "is responsible for all storage costs incurred throughout the duration
of the review." That's what "rebuttable" costs in practice while you're rebutting it: the
goods sit, and the bill runs whether you eventually win the case or lose it.

## How to respond, and the clock you're on

From CBP's
[operational guidance](https://www.cbp.gov/sites/default/files/2026-06/fld_importer_guidance_final_06092026_0.pdf):

| Stage | Your window | Your options |
|---|---|---|
| Detention notice | **30 days** | Export or destroy the goods, or request a review. An *applicability review* argues the goods aren't from the XUAR or a listed entity at all; an *exception* argues they are, but meet the three-part test above. |
| Extension | Up to two, at CBP's discretion | No more than **90 calendar days** in total from the detention notice. |
| No action taken | 30 days | The shipment "is deemed excluded." |
| Exclusion notice | **180 days** | Export or destroy, or file a protest under 19 U.S.C. § 1514. |
| Protest denied | **180 days** | File a civil action in the U.S. Court of International Trade. |

CBP may also require a single-transaction bond of three times the value of the detained goods
while the review runs.

## What this means practically

- **Check the Entity List before you check anything else.** It changes faster than most
  compliance calendars assume, and it now reaches beyond the region its name suggests.
- **Build your traceability file before a shipment is detained, not after.** Thirty days —
  ninety at most, if CBP grants extensions — is not long to assemble clear and convincing
  evidence from a standing start.
- **A denial isn't necessarily final, but it's expensive either way.** An exclusion can be
  protested and a denied protest taken to court, but the storage bill is yours throughout,
  and every month under review is a month the goods aren't being sold.
