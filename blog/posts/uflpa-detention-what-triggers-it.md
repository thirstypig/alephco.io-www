People describe the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act as a ban. It isn't one. It's a
presumption — and the distinction matters enormously for what you actually have to do
about it.

Checked against CBP's own guidance on **9 March 2027**.

## The mechanism, precisely

CBP enforces "the rebuttable presumption that goods mined, produced, or manufactured wholly
or in part in the XUAR, or by an entity on the UFLPA Entity List, are prohibited from U.S.
importation under 19 U.S.C. § 1307." The presumption is rebuttable — but the bar is high.
The importer must overcome it "by establishing, by clear and convincing evidence, that the
good... was not mined, produced, or manufactured wholly or in part by forced labor."

Clear and convincing evidence is a real, higher legal standard than the "preponderance of
the evidence" bar used in most civil disputes. That's the part people underestimate: this
isn't a checkbox but a genuine evidentiary burden, and it sits entirely on the importer.

## Who's actually on the list

The UFLPA Entity List is published by DHS, and it's growing fast. The most recent expansion
— 43 companies added in a single update — brought the list to **187 entities**, roughly a
30% jump and the largest single expansion since UFLPA took effect in 2022. The new additions
span aluminum, apparel, copper, cotton, and tomato-product supply chains, and notably some
of the newly listed entities are headquartered outside Xinjiang entirely — in Shandong,
Jiangsu, and Henan. Being outside the region named in the law's title doesn't put a supplier
outside its reach.

## What this actually cost one company

**VSUN**, a solar module brand under Japanese parent Abalance (itself acquired by Toyo
Corporation in 2025), had its solar modules detained by CBP on 11 January 2026. Abalance
disclosed the detention publicly weeks later, projecting a hit of roughly ¥3–5 billion
(about $18–31 million) to annual earnings — the detained modules would have to be sold at a
discount in markets outside the US instead. The company's public statement: "sufficient
evidence to allow for an exception could not be confirmed," while it worked with US legal
counsel to make its case, maintaining that it and VSUN "do not use raw materials
originating from the Uyghur region."

That's what "rebuttable" costs in practice while you're rebutting it: the goods sit, and the
business impact starts immediately, whether you eventually win the case or lose it.

## What this means practically

- **Check the Entity List before you check anything else.** It changes faster than most
  compliance calendars assume, and it now reaches beyond the region its name suggests.
- **Build your traceability file before a shipment is detained, not after.** The clear-and-
  convincing standard rewards documentation that already existed, not documentation
  assembled under deadline pressure.
- **A denial isn't necessarily final, but it's expensive either way.** Even a released
  shipment has already absorbed the delay and the discounted-market cost of the goods
  sitting in limbo.
