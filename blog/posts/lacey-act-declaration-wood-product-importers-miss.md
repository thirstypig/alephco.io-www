We originally scoped this post as "furniture and paper importers." That was wrong, and
it's worth explaining why: plain paper products were pulled off the declaration schedule
back in 2009 and haven't come back. If your product actually contains wood, though, the
declaration below almost certainly applies to you now — and for a lot of products, it
didn't before December 2024.

Everything below is linked to the statute, APHIS's own pages, the Federal Register, or a
DOJ record. Checked against those sources on **10 September 2026**. If you're reading
this much later, check the links rather than the text.

## What the declaration actually requires

Two different parts of the statute do the work, and they're easy to blur together.

[16 U.S.C. § 3372(a)(2)(B)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title16-section3372&num=0&edition=prelim)
is the underlying prohibition: it's unlawful to import, buy, sell, receive, or transport a
plant that was taken, possessed, transported, or sold in violation of a State or foreign
law that protects plants or governs their harvest, royalties and taxes, or export.

[§ 3372(f)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title16-section3372&num=0&edition=prelim)
is the declaration itself. It's unlawful to import a covered plant product without filing
one that states:

- the **scientific name**, including the genus and species, of any plant in the shipment;
- the **country from which the plant was taken**;
- the **value** of the importation; and
- the **quantity**, including the unit of measure.

APHIS's [requirements page](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/plant-imports/file-lacey-act-declaration/requirements)
adds the standard shipment details on top — HTS code, bill of lading, container number,
importer and consignee — and wants quantity in metric units.

Not knowing isn't a way out. Under § 3372(f)(2), when the species used in a product varies
and you don't know which one went in, the declaration must name each species that may have
been used; the same rule applies to the country of harvest. The one narrow alternative is
for composite material — wood that's been broken down and re-formed, like particleboard or
MDF. If you can't determine the species through due care, APHIS lets you use a
[Special Use Designation](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/plant-imports/file-lacey-act-declaration/frequently-asked-questions)
in place of genus and species.

## Why "furniture and paper" was the wrong framing

APHIS's Phase VII took effect on **1 December 2024**, and it's the broadest expansion yet:
declarations are now required for "all remaining plant product Harmonized Tariff Schedule
(HTS) codes that are not 100-percent composite materials"
([APHIS](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/news/program-update/aphis-will-implement-lacey-act-phase-vii-requirements-dec-1)).
The [Federal Register notice](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/05/31/2024-11901/implementation-of-revised-lacey-act-provisions)
lists the codes, and APHIS's own examples reach well past lumber: plywood and laminated
wood, handbags, tools, cork, bamboo and rattan, footwear, furniture, sporting goods, and
some essential oils
([APHIS update](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/news/program-update/aphis-will-implement-lacey-act-phase-vii-requirements-dec-1),
[APHIS FAQ](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/plant-imports/file-lacey-act-declaration/frequently-asked-questions)).
Furniture is squarely, currently in scope.

Plain paper (HTS Chapter 48) is not. It was on the phase-in schedule APHIS published in
[February 2009](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2009/02/03/E9-2232/implementation-of-revised-lacey-act-provisions),
then pulled in the [September 2009 revision](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2009/09/02/E9-21216/implementation-of-revised-lacey-act-provisions)
onto a list of chapters "under consideration for subsequent phases." No phase since has
added it, and Phase VII excludes codes that are 100-percent composite — a category APHIS
says includes paper and paperboard.

Two catches worth knowing:

- **Composite material inside a covered product still counts.** APHIS's
  [FAQ](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/plant-imports/file-lacey-act-declaration/frequently-asked-questions)
  says composite plant material — paperboard and particleboard included — still needs a
  declaration unless it's otherwise exempted. If your wooden cabinet has a particleboard
  back, that's where the Special Use Designation earns its keep.
- **A covered HTS code isn't the whole test.** APHIS
  [said in 2009](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2009/09/02/E9-21216/implementation-of-revised-lacey-act-provisions)
  that a declaration isn't required for products with no wood or other plant content. A
  metal hammer and a wooden-handled hammer share an HTS chapter; only the second one needs
  the paperwork.

## What ignoring this actually looks like

On 27 April 2026, **Boise Cascade Company**
[pleaded guilty to a felony Lacey Act violation](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/boise-cascade-pleads-guilty-and-sentenced-violating-lacey-act-its-role-timber-trafficking)
and was fined **$6,382,000** — twice the gross profits it made from the illegal wood.

Over the 2018–2021 sales years, its Pompano, Florida location bought more than $30 million
of hardwood plywood from a supplier, Horizon Plywood. From about 2019, DOJ says, Boise
Cascade did so knowing — "including actions manifesting willful blindness" — that the
plywood had been illegally imported from China. Horizon transshipped product through
Malaysia to hide its origin, falsified its import declarations, and evaded countervailing
and anti-dumping duties. And after federal agents executed a search warrant at Horizon's
South Florida warehouse in January 2021, Boise Cascade still placed at least 10 new orders
in the next two weeks.

Notice who filed what. Boise Cascade didn't file the false declarations — Horizon did.
Boise Cascade was the buyer, and the Lacey Act reaches buyers: its prohibitions cover
purchasing, receiving, selling, and transporting, not just importing.

The older, more famous case is Gibson Guitar (2012): a
[criminal enforcement agreement](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/gibson-guitar-corp-agrees-resolve-investigation-lacey-act-violations)
that deferred prosecution — not a conviction — over ebony from Madagascar and rosewood and
ebony from India. Worth getting the number right if you cite it: the headline $300,000 is
the penalty alone. Gibson also paid $50,000 in community service to the National Fish and
Wildlife Foundation and withdrew its claims to seized wood, including Madagascar ebony from
shipments with a total invoice value of $261,844 — roughly $612,000 all in.

Boise Cascade is the more relevant precedent for most wood-product importers today: it
involves ordinary building material and falsified import declarations, not a
celebrity-adjacent instrument maker — and the liability landed on the customer.

## What this means practically

- **If your product has real wood content, assume Phase VII applies.** Check your HTS codes
  against APHIS's
  [implementation schedule](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/plant-imports/lacey-act/implementation-schedule),
  then confirm the exact species and country of harvest with your supplier — in writing,
  before the shipment moves.
- **A supplier's paperwork is not self-verifying.** Boise Cascade's exposure came from
  continuing to buy after it had every reason to stop, up to and including a federal search
  warrant at its supplier's warehouse.
- **The declaration is electronic only.** As of 1 January 2026, APHIS
  [no longer accepts paper PPQ 505 or 505B forms](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/plant-imports/file-lacey-act-declaration);
  you file through CBP's ACE or APHIS's Lacey Act Web Governance System (LAWGS).
