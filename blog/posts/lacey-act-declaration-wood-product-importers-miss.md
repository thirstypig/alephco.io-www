We originally scoped this post as "furniture and paper importers." That was wrong, and
worth explaining why: plain paper products were pulled out of scope back in 2009 and stay
there. If your product actually contains wood, though, the declaration below almost
certainly applies to you now — and it didn't a few years ago.

Checked against APHIS's own implementation schedule on **9 March 2027**.

## What the declaration actually requires

Under 16 U.S.C. § 3372(f), importing plants or plant products taken in violation of
underlying law is unlawful, and the required import declaration must include the **genus
and species** of any plant in the shipment and the **country from which it was taken** —
plus quantity, product value, and standard shipment details. Where the exact species or
country isn't known, APHIS requires listing *every* one that could plausibly apply. Blank
fields aren't an option.

## Why "furniture and paper" was the wrong framing

APHIS's Phase VII, effective **1 December 2024**, is the broadest expansion yet: it covers
"all remaining plant product Harmonized Tariff Schedule (HTS) codes that are not
100-percent composite materials" — explicitly including furniture, plywood, laminated
wood, handbags, tools, cork, bamboo and rattan, footwear, and even some sporting goods and
essential oils. Furniture is squarely, currently in scope.

Plain paper (HTS Chapter 48) is not — it was slated for coverage originally, then removed
when APHIS revised the schedule back in 2009, and it has stayed out since. Only paper or
paperboard products that fall under wood-product HTS Chapter 44 are captured. If your
product is genuinely wood — furniture, flooring, plywood, veneer — this applies to you
regardless of what you call it. If it's genuinely just paper, it likely doesn't.

## What ignoring this actually looks like

In April 2026, **Boise Cascade Company** pleaded guilty to a felony Lacey Act violation and
was fined **$6,382,000** — set at twice the illegal profit — for a scheme that ran from
2018 to 2021. Its Florida operation bought more than $30 million in hardwood plywood from a
supplier it knew was illegally transshipping Chinese plywood through Malaysia to disguise
its real origin and dodge anti-dumping duties. Boise Cascade kept placing orders with that
supplier even after learning federal agents had already executed a search warrant there.

The older, more famous case is Gibson Guitar (2012) — a deferred prosecution agreement, not
a conviction, over ebony and rosewood sourced from Madagascar and India. Worth getting the
number right if you cite it: the headline "$300,000" is the penalty alone; total exposure,
including a $50,000 community-service payment and $261,844 in forfeited wood, ran closer to
$612,000.

Boise Cascade is the more relevant precedent for most wood-product importers today — it's
about falsified country-of-origin paperwork on ordinary building material, not a
celebrity-adjacent instrument maker.

## What this means practically

- **If your product has real wood content, assume Phase VII applies** and confirm the exact
  species and country of harvest with your supplier — in writing, before the shipment
  moves.
- **A supplier's country-of-origin claim is not self-verifying.** Boise Cascade's exposure
  came from continuing to trust a supplier's paperwork after it had reason not to.
- **The declaration is now filed electronically only** — paper PPQ 505/505B forms stopped
  being accepted as of January 2026.
