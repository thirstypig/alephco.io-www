The Prop 65 list is not static, and an addition to it can change what your existing,
unchanged product requires. Worth understanding how chemicals get on, because one of the
routes produces additions that never looked like a California rulemaking at all.

Checked against the statute on **6 September 2026**. Background:
[Prop 65 for importers](/blog/prop-65-for-importers-who-it-applies.html).

## You have twelve months, and that is the whole grace period

[§25249.10(b)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=HSC&sectionNum=25249.10)
exempts an exposure "that takes place less than twelve months subsequent to the listing of
the chemical in question".

Twelve months from listing. Not from when you found out, not from when your supplier told
you, not from when you next revise artwork. If a chemical in your product is listed today,
the warning obligation attaches a year from today whether or not anyone in your business
knows.

That single sentence is why monitoring the list is an operational task rather than a legal
curiosity.

## Three ways onto the list, and the third is the sneaky one

[§25249.8(b)](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=HSC&sectionNum=25249.8)
says a chemical is known to the state to cause cancer or reproductive toxicity if:

1. **The state's qualified experts** conclude it has been clearly shown, through
   scientifically valid testing according to generally accepted principles, to cause cancer
   or reproductive toxicity; **or**
2. **A body considered authoritative** by those experts "has formally identified it" as
   causing cancer or reproductive toxicity; **or**
3. **An agency of the state or federal government** "has formally required it to be labeled
   or identified as causing cancer or reproductive toxicity."

Routes 2 and 3 are the ones to notice. A listing can follow from a determination made
somewhere else entirely — by an authoritative scientific body, or by a federal agency
imposing its own labelling requirement. The triggering event may have had nothing to do
with California and may not have been reported as a Prop 65 development anywhere you read.

§25249.8(a) also requires the list to be revised and republished "at least once per year",
which is the floor, not the cadence.

## What this means for a catalogue

The uncomfortable implication: **your compliance position can change without your product
changing.** No reformulation, no new supplier, no new market. A chemical that has been in
your product for a decade gets listed, and twelve months later you need a warning.

So the monitoring question is not "has our product changed?" but "has the list moved under
it?" Those need different systems. The first is a supplier question. The second is a
calendar.

## What a workable process looks like

1. **Know what is in your products, by chemical.** You cannot compare a catalogue to a list
   if one side of the comparison doesn't exist. This is the same
   [supplier declaration problem](/blog/getting-a-pfas-declaration-out-of-an.html) that
   PFAS reporting runs into, and the same answer serves both.
2. **Watch the list, not the news.** Additions arrive via three routes and only some of them
   generate coverage.
3. **On an addition, ask one question:** does any product contain it? If the answer takes
   more than a day, that is the finding, not the listing.
4. **Diarise twelve months from the listing date**, and work back from it — artwork, stock
   in transit, marketplace listings.
5. **Decide per SKU:** warn, or establish you are below the level. The second route needs
   [an exposure assessment](/blog/exposure-assessment-without-a-toxicologist.html) and the
   burden is yours.

## The part that catches people

Twelve months feels generous, and it is — right up until the moment you discover the
chemical is in a component you never specified, supplied by a factory you no longer buy
from, in stock that is already on a boat. The clock does not care, and it started when the
chemical was listed rather than when you learned any of this.

Aleph tracks which chemicals are associated with each product, so a listing becomes a query
rather than a survey.
