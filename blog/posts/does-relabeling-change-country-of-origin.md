Every importer facing a Section 301 tariff eventually asks some version of the same
question: if we finish the product somewhere else, does that change where it's "from"? CBP
has an actual legal test for this, and it is stricter than most people expect.

Checked against CBP's own rulings on **9 March 2027**.

## The test, in CBP's own words

Country of origin turns on **substantial transformation** — whether an article has "been
substantially transformed into a new and different article of commerce with a name,
character, or use distinct from that of the article or articles from which it was so
transformed" (19 U.S.C. § 2518(4)(B)). CBP's own case law adds the practical gloss: "a
substantial transformation will not result from a minor manufacturing or combining process
that leaves the identity of the article intact."

For marking purposes specifically, [19 CFR § 102.11](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/102.11)
runs a tiered test — wholly obtained in one country, or made there entirely from domestic
materials, or each foreign input undergoes a qualifying tariff classification change.

## Two real rulings, opposite outcomes

CBP's own ruling **HQ H261693** (2015) is the clean "no" case. A manufacturer assembled
finished solar panels from Malaysian or Korean solar cells, in Korea or Poland, in a nine-step
process taking less than one day. CBP's finding: the solar cells "impart the essential
character of the solar panels," so the country of origin stayed wherever the cells were
actually made — not wherever they were snapped into a frame.

**HQ H095409**, quoted inside that same ruling, is the "yes" case. A US manufacturer made
solar cells from bare glass tubes — depositing thin films of chemicals, a process that took
five of the six and a half days needed to build the finished panel, with 43% US cost content
and US-based R&D. CBP called that "making a product with a new name, character, and use."
Real transformation, not a finishing step.

The difference isn't subjective. It comes down to whether the process changed what the
thing *is*, not just where it was touched last.

## What ignoring that test actually costs

In December 2025, **Ceratizit USA LLC** — a Charlotte, NC tungsten carbide distributor —
agreed to pay **$54.4 million** to resolve False Claims Act allegations that it mislabeled
Chinese-origin tungsten carbide as Taiwan-origin from roughly 2020 to 2024, specifically to
avoid Section 301 duties, alongside related misclassification and marking violations. DOJ
called it the largest customs-duty-evasion settlement of its kind on record. It started as a
whistleblower case — the individual who filed it received nearly $9.75 million of the
settlement.

That's the real shape of the risk: not a disputed classification argument, but a False
Claims Act case with a nine-figure number attached, because "we relabeled it" and "we
transformed it" are two legally distinct claims.

## What this means practically

- **If your process is assembly, packaging, or labeling only, assume origin hasn't
  moved** until a real CBP ruling on a comparable product says otherwise.
- **A CBP ruling request (a formal binding ruling) is cheap insurance** against a decision
  this expensive to get wrong.
- **"Transshipment" and "substantial transformation" are not the same defense.** Routing
  goods through a third country changes nothing about their real origin — only a genuine
  manufacturing change does.
