Every importer facing a Section 301 tariff eventually asks some version of the same
question: if we finish the product somewhere else, does that change where it's "from"? CBP
has an actual legal test for this, and it's stricter than most people expect.

Checked against CBP's own rulings and regulations on **10 September 2026**. If you're
reading this much later, check the links rather than the text.

## The test, in CBP's own words

For marking, the rule is
[19 CFR § 134.1(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-134/subpart-A/section-134.1):
"Further work or material added to an article in another country must effect a substantial
transformation in order to render such other country the 'country of origin' within the
meaning of this part."

CBP uses the same test for Section 301. In a March 2026 binding ruling,
[HQ H350697](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H350697), it says so directly: "The substantial
transformation analysis is applicable when determining the country of origin for purposes
of applying Section 301 measures."

What counts as a substantial transformation? The same ruling puts it this way: "whether an
article emerges from a process with a new name, character or use, different from that
possessed by the article prior to processing" — a determination "based on the totality of
the evidence." And the courts have set a floor that CBP repeats in its rulings, citing
*United States v. Gibson-Thomsen Co.* (1940) and *National Hand Tool Corp. v. United
States* (Fed. Cir. 1992): "A substantial transformation will not result from a minor
manufacturing or combining process that leaves the identity of the article intact."

⚠️ **Two exceptions.** First, textiles and apparel have their own origin rules:
[19 CFR § 102.21](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-102/section-102.21)
"will control the determination of the country of origin of imported textile and apparel
products for purposes of the Customs laws." If that's what you import, start there instead.

Second, goods from Canada or Mexico. Section 134.1(b) continues: "for a
good of a NAFTA or USMCA country, the marking rules set forth in part 102 of this chapter
… will determine the country of origin" (see also
[§ 102.0](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-102/section-102.0)). Those
rules, in [19 CFR § 102.11](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-102/section-102.11),
start from a tariff-shift test: the good is wholly obtained or produced in one country, or
produced there exclusively from domestic materials, or each foreign material "undergoes an
applicable change in tariff classification set out in § 102.20." When none of those settles
it, fallback rules take over, starting with the material that "imparts the essential
character to the good."

## Two real rulings, opposite outcomes

Both of these are *government-procurement* determinations under the Trade Agreements Act,
not Section 301 rulings. The procurement version of the test is written into statute at
[19 U.S.C. § 2518(4)(B)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title19-section2518&num=0&edition=prelim):
an article must be "substantially transformed into a new and different article of commerce
with a name, character, or use distinct from that of the article or articles from which it
was so transformed." The rulings are still worth reading, because CBP spells out its
reasoning in unusual detail and leans on the same case law (*National Hand Tool*, for one)
that it cites in Section 301 rulings like H350697.

**HQ H261693** (September 2015) is the clean "no" case. Hanwha assembled finished solar
modules in Korea or Poland from Malaysian or Korean solar cells, in a nine-step process
that took "less than one day." The cells were slightly more than half the cost of the
module; where Malaysian cells were used, Korean content was at most 8.6% and Polish content
0%. CBP's finding: "the solar cells impart the essential character of the solar panels."
So the country of origin stayed wherever the cells were made — Malaysia or Korea — not
wherever they were framed and laminated. The full text is in the
[Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2015-09-22/pdf/2015-24082.pdf)
and on [CBP's rulings database](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H261693).

**[HQ H095409](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H095409)** (September 2010), which H261693
walks through as its point of comparison, is the "yes" case. Solyndra built finished panels
in California. The key step was making the solar cells themselves: "depositing thin films
of chemicals on the inside of glass tubes," which took five of the six and a half days
needed to build a panel. Forty-three percent of the parts' cost content was US-origin, and
all research and development was in California. CBP found that "turning bare glass tubes
into functional solar cells in the United States constituted making a product with a new
name, character, and use."

CBP weighs the totality of the circumstances, case by case, and says outright that "no one
factor is determinative." But in both rulings the deciding question was the same: did the
process change what the thing *is*, or just where it was touched last?

## What getting origin wrong can cost

On 18 December 2025, the Justice Department
[announced](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/ceratizit-usa-llc-agrees-pay-544m-settle-false-claims-act-allegations-relating-evaded-0)
that **Ceratizit USA LLC**, a Charlotte, North Carolina–based distributor of tungsten
carbide products, had agreed to pay **$54.4 million** to resolve False Claims Act
allegations. The government alleged that from August 2020 through March 2024, Ceratizit
knew its products "had been manufactured in China and transshipped to Taiwan," and
misrepresented to CBP that they originated in Taiwan to avoid Section 301 tariffs.

The settlement also resolved allegations that Ceratizit used the wrong tariff
classification from June 2015 and imported goods that weren't marked with their country of
origin. DOJ notes that these are "allegations only and there has been no determination of
liability." The case began as a whistleblower suit, and the whistleblower will receive
approximately $9.75 million of the settlement.

Notice what that case *wasn't*: a close call about transformation. The allegation was that
the goods were simply routed through Taiwan. "We relabeled it" and "we transformed it" are
two different legal claims, and an eight-figure settlement is what confusing them can cost.

## What this means practically

- **If your process is assembly, packaging, or labeling only, assume origin hasn't
  moved** until a CBP ruling on a comparable product says otherwise. Real manufacturing is
  a different story: in H350697, CBP found a smartwatch was a product of Vietnam because
  its circuit boards were manufactured there.
- **Ask CBP before you import, not after.** Under
  [19 CFR § 177.1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-177/subpart-A/section-177.1),
  a ruling can generally be requested "only with respect to prospective transactions." Once
  issued, it "is binding on all Customs Service personnel"
  ([§ 177.9](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-19/chapter-I/part-177/subpart-A/section-177.9)),
  on the assumption that the facts you gave CBP are "accurate and complete in every material
  respect."
- **"Transshipment" and "substantial transformation" are not the same defense.** CBP's own
  [Section 301 FAQ](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/entry-summary/section-301-trade-remedies/faqs)
  is blunt: "Section 301 duties are based on country of origin, not country of export."
  Routing goods through a third country doesn't change their origin; only a substantial
  transformation does.
