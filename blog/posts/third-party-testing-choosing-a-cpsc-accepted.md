If your product is a children's product, its certificate has to rest on testing at a
CPSC-accepted third party lab — see [GCC vs CPC](/blog/gcc-vs-cpc-which-certificate-does-your.html)
for which certificate you need. Choosing that lab has one trap in it that costs people a
whole test round.

Checked against the regulations on **6 September 2026**.

## Acceptance is per-rule, not per-lab

A lab is not simply "CPSC-accepted". Under
[16 CFR §1112.13](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/part-1112) a body applies with a
**statement of scope identifying the specific CPSC rules and test methods** it seeks
acceptance for, backed by ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation from an accreditation body that is a
signatory to the ILAC Mutual Recognition Arrangement.

So the question is never "are you CPSC-accepted?" It is **"are you accepted for the rule my
product is subject to?"** A lab accepted for lead content is not thereby accepted for small
parts, or for a specific toy standard. Ask for the scope, and check it against the rules you
named on your certificate.

## Three kinds of body, and why it matters who owns it

§1112.11 recognises three:

- **Independent** — not owned, managed or controlled by a manufacturer, private labeler or
  government.
- **Firewalled** — owned or managed by a manufacturer or private labeler, operating under
  protective procedures.
- **Governmental** — owned or controlled wholly or partly by a government entity.

A body must apply as **firewalled** where manufacturer or private-labeler ownership reaches
10% or more, directly or indirectly, or where there is a common parent or appointment
authority. Firewalled bodies must submit policies, training records, organisational charts
and reporting lines showing protection against **undue influence** on test results.

This is worth knowing if a supplier offers to arrange testing at "their" lab. That can be
entirely legitimate — firewalled bodies exist precisely for it — but the arrangement has
conditions, and you are the certifier relying on the result.

## How often you have to retest

[16 CFR §1107.21](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/part-1107) sets periodic testing
intervals, and the interval depends on what else you have in place:

| Approach | Minimum interval |
|---|---|
| Standard periodic testing plan (§1107.21(b)) | At least **once a year** |
| With a production testing plan (§1107.21(c)) | At least **once every two years** |
| Production tested at an ISO/IEC 17025 lab (§1107.21(d)) | At least **once every three years** |

§1107.21(b)(2) lists ten factors for setting the interval within those limits — production
volume, injury potential, and how close your measured results sit to the regulatory limit
among them. A result that just scrapes under a limit argues for testing more often, not
less, and that reasoning is the kind of thing the file should show.

## Material change resets it

Under §1107.23(a), a material change in design, manufacturing process or **component
sourcing** that could affect compliance requires a **new Children's Product Certificate**,
supported by new third party testing.

"Component sourcing" is the one that catches importers. A factory switching a pigment
supplier, a fastener or a coating is a material change even though the product looks
identical and the model number never moved. Nothing generates a notification — you find out
by asking, which means the standing question to a supplier is not "has anything changed?"
but "what changed since [date]?"

## Keep the records for five years

§1107.26 lists what must be kept: certificates, certification test records, periodic test
records and plans, representative sampling documentation, descriptions of material changes
with their test results, and undue influence training records. All of it for **five years**
(§1107.26(b)).

Five years is longer than most supplier relationships and much longer than most inboxes.
Test reports living in an email thread with a factory you no longer buy from is the normal
state of affairs, and it is not a record system.

## The practical order

1. Name the rules your product is subject to — that list is on your certificate anyway.
2. Ask candidate labs for their **scope** and check your rules are in it.
3. Ask whether they are independent, firewalled or governmental, and whose ownership is
   involved.
4. Set your periodic interval deliberately, using the §1107.21(b)(2) factors, and write
   down why.
5. Put a standing "what changed?" question to suppliers, and treat a component swap as a
   retest trigger.
6. Store the report against the product, with its date, where it will still be in five
   years.
