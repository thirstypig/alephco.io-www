MoCRA is two obligations, not one, and they land on different parties with different
clocks. Importers of cosmetics get caught out because they satisfy one and assume it covers
the other.

Checked against 21 U.S.C. §364c and FDA guidance on **6 September 2026**.

## The two obligations

| | Facility registration | Product listing |
|---|---|---|
| Who | The owner or operator of a facility that **manufactures or processes** cosmetics for US distribution | The **responsible person** |
| Cycle | **Biennial** renewal | **Annual** updates |
| Statute | [§364c(a)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/21/364c) | §364c(c) |

They are not alternatives. A brand that has its products made by a contract manufacturer
still has a listing obligation even though it operates no facility, and the manufacturer
still registers even though it is not the responsible person.

## Foreign facilities are in scope

This is the one importers most often get wrong. §364c(a)(1)(A) applies to every person who
owns or operates a facility engaged in manufacturing or processing a cosmetic product **for
distribution in the United States** — not only facilities located in the US.

§364c(b)(2)(B) confirms it by requiring, for a foreign facility, the contact for its **United
States agent**. If your products are made overseas, that factory has a registration
obligation and you will need its registration number.

New facilities register **within 60 days** of first engaging in the activity, or 60 days
after the original deadline, whichever is later (§364c(a)(1)(B)).

## Who the "responsible person" is

FDA's definition follows the label: the **manufacturer, packer, or distributor whose name
appears on the label** under section 609(a) of the FD&C Act or section 4(a) of the Fair
Packaging and Labeling Act.

For most importers that is you. If your name is on the pack as the distributor, the listing
obligation is yours, and it does not transfer to the factory because they registered.

## The 120-day rule for new products

§364c(c)(2) requires listing of a cosmetic product first marketed after 29 December 2022
**within 120 days of marketing it in interstate commerce**, and §364c(c)(5) requires updates
annually.

120 days is short enough to matter for a brand that launches regularly, and the clock starts
at marketing — not at the point somebody remembers to do the paperwork.

## Renewals do not fall on a common date

Registrations renew biennially, and the renewal date runs from **your** initial registration,
not from a fixed calendar date shared across the industry. A facility first registered in
February renews in February.

FDA has added **REGISTRATION STATUS** and **RENEWAL DATE** fields to the Cosmetics Direct
portal, announced 11 February 2026, which makes this checkable rather than calculable. Check
each facility you rely on, including the foreign ones.

## The small business exemption, and its holes

Certain small businesses are exempt from registration and listing — but **not** if they
manufacture or process cosmetic products that:

- regularly come into contact with mucous membranes of the eye under customary use,
- are injected,
- are intended for internal use, or
- are intended to alter appearance for **more than 24 hours** under customary use, where
  removal by the consumer is not part of that use.

Read that last one against your catalogue before relying on the exemption. Long-wear and
semi-permanent products are exactly the category it excludes.

## A short checklist

1. For each product, identify the responsible person by looking at the label.
2. For each product, identify every facility that manufactures or processes it — including
   overseas — and confirm each is registered.
3. Check renewal dates per facility in Cosmetics Direct; they are not synchronised.
4. Put a 120-day listing task in your launch process, triggered by first marketing.
5. Diarise annual listing updates, and treat a formulation, labelling or contact change as
   an early trigger.
