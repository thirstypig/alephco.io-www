The uncomfortable thing about a records request is that it tests something you cannot fix
once it arrives. Either the records exist and can be produced, or they don't and can't.

Checked on **6 September 2026**.

## The number that should design your system

[21 CFR §1.510](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/section-1.510) requires FSVP records
be made available to FDA **within 24 hours** of request, kept at least two years, legible,
and stored to prevent deterioration.

Twenty-four hours is the constraint everything else has to satisfy. It rules out
reconstruction, it rules out asking a supplier, and it rules out one person being on
holiday. A programme that is complete but scattered fails this even though nothing is
missing.

Retention periods to design against, together:

| Records | Period | Source |
|---|---|---|
| FSVP | 2 years | [§1.510(c)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/section-1.510) |
| Children's product testing and certificates | **5 years** | [16 CFR §1107.26](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/part-1107) |
| Canadian traceability documents | **6 years** after the end of the year | [CCPSA s.13(2)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-1.68/page-1.html) |

Build to the longest one. Running three policies produces gaps at the boundaries, and the
boundaries are where the questions land.

## What is actually requested

Rarely "everything". Usually a chain, for one product:

1. The **certificate**, naming each applicable rule separately
   ([§1110.11](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/part-1110)).
2. The **test report** it rests on, and the lab's acceptance scope.
3. The **dates** — manufacture, test, most recent test — and how they relate to your
   periodic interval.
4. The **supplier evidence**: declarations, approvals, verification activities.
5. Whatever shows the **chain is unbroken** since: material change checks, reevaluations.

Item 5 is where files fail. The first four are usually collected once, at launch. The fifth
is continuous, and continuous is what nobody set up.

## Prepare the boring way

- **Pick one product and run a drill.** Ask for the five items above and time it. The result
  is your real readiness, and it is usually a surprise. If it takes a week, that is the
  finding — not the paperwork.
- **Know who can produce them.** A single point of failure who is travelling is a 24-hour
  problem.
- **Keep documents against products, not in correspondence.** Email threads outlive nothing
  and are searchable by the wrong keys.
- **Store the *reasoning*, not only the artefacts.** Why this interval, why this alternative
  to an onsite audit, why no hazard requires a control. Regulations repeatedly permit a
  determination — and repeatedly require it to be **written**.

## During a request

- **Answer what was asked.** Volunteering unrelated material widens the conversation.
- **Be accurate about what does not exist.** A record you cannot produce is a finding; a
  record you misdescribe is a different and worse problem.
- **Do not create documents to fill the gap.** Backdating turns a compliance failure into
  something else entirely.
- **Log what you provided, and when.** You will need it if there is a follow-up.
- **Fix the gap, and record when you fixed it.** Timing of good-faith measures is expressly
  a mitigating factor in some regimes — see
  [60-day notices](/blog/you-received-a-60-day-notice-now.html).

## Afterwards

Every request teaches you where the file is thin. The value is in fixing the *class* of
problem rather than the instance — if the test date was hard to find for one SKU, it is hard
to find for all of them, and you have been shown that for free.
