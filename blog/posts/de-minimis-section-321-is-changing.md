People still ask whether de minimis is "going away." It already went. What's left to explain
is which of three separate government actions actually did it, and why the exemption isn't
coming back the way a paused rule would.

Checked against the Federal Register on **9 March 2027**.

## What de minimis used to mean

Section 321 of the Tariff Act let one shipment per person per day, valued at $800 or less,
enter duty-free with a minimal customs process. For low-value e-commerce — a $15 phone
case, a $40 t-shirt — that made the whole transaction cheaper to run than the tariff would
have been to collect.

## Three actions, not one

**Executive Order 14324**, signed 30 July 2025, is the one most people know about:

> The duty-free de minimis exemption provided under 19 U.S.C. 1321(a)(2)(C) shall no longer
> apply to any shipment of articles not covered by 50 U.S.C. 1702(b), regardless of value,
> country of origin, mode of transportation, or method of entry.

It took effect 29 August 2025. CBP's implementation notice followed a week later, confirming
the cutoff for everything except a temporary postal carve-out.

The part that changes how you should read this: **Congress did the same thing independently.**
The "One Big Beautiful Bill Act," signed 4 July 2025, repeals the Section 321(a)(2) exemption
by statute — for every country, effective **1 July 2027** — and adds civil penalties (up to
$10,000 per violation) for using de minimis to import goods that break other customs law. If
a future administration rescinded the executive order tomorrow, the exemption would still be
dead in eighteen months by law.

Then, in June 2026, CBP closed the remaining gap with a regulation, not just an order — an
interim final rule amending **19 CFR § 10.151(b)** to suspend the exemption for everything
except the international postal network, "until such time as CBP determines that the
application of the exemption is no longer inconsistent with the purpose of 19 U.S.C.
1321(a)." Even the postal carve-out has a closing date on it now.

⚠️ One place NOT to check: CBP's own Section 321 program page still describes the $800
exemption in the present tense, with no mention of any of this. If you or your broker land
there instead of the Federal Register, you'll read the old rule as current. It isn't.

## What this actually costs

Shein and Temu didn't wait to find out. Both sent customers near-identical notices in April
2025 — "we will be making price adjustments starting April 25, 2025" — ahead of a
China-specific suspension that preceded the global one, as low-value postal shipments from
China faced a duty of up to 120% or a flat per-item fee. The market response was immediate:
one traffic analysis found Temu's US audience fell as much as 62% and Shein's 47% in the
weeks after, with both still running roughly 30% below their pre-tariff baseline a year
later. In the two weeks *before* the change, the opposite happened — shoppers front-loaded
orders, and both companies saw a short revenue spike of 38–60% year over year.

That's the shape of the cost: not a line-item on an invoice, but a demand curve that moves
as soon as the exemption a business model was built on disappears.

## What this means practically

- **Model your landed cost as if de minimis doesn't exist**, because for nearly all
  commercial shipments today, it doesn't.
- **The postal carve-out is not a long-term plan.** It has its own compliance dates already
  moving toward closure.
- **If a state or vendor cites "the $800 exemption" as current, ask which source they're
  reading** — the Federal Register record and a stale program page currently disagree.
