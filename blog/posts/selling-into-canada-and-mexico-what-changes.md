The instinct when expanding north or south is to treat it as the US programme with a
translated label. Both countries break that assumption, in different places.

Checked on **6 September 2026**. Canadian requirements below are from the statute; Mexican
requirements are summarised from US government guidance, and the Mexican standards
themselves are the authority — see the caveat at the end.

## Canada: the clock is the difference

The [Canada Consumer Product Safety Act](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-1.68/page-1.html)
prohibits manufacturing or importing consumer products that do not meet the regulations
(s.6), and separately prohibits importing a product that is a danger to human health or
safety or is subject to a recall order (s.7).

That much will feel familiar. These will not:

**Incident reporting on a two-day clock.** Under s.14(2) you must provide all information in
your control regarding an incident **within two days** of becoming aware of it, followed by
a written report **within ten days** under s.14(3).

Two days is short. It is short enough that the process has to exist before the incident,
because there is no time to design one afterwards. If your US practice is to investigate
first and report when you understand the problem, that sequence does not fit.

**Six years of traceability records.** Section 13(1) requires records showing the name and
address of the person you obtained the product from, or sold it to. Section 13(2) requires
those documents be kept for **six years after the end of the year** to which they relate.

Six years is longer than the two years FSVP requires and longer than the five years for
children's product testing records. If you are building one retention policy, Canada sets
the floor.

**Documents at importation.** Section 13(5) requires an importer to provide prescribed
documents **no later than at the time of the product's importation** — not on request
afterwards.

## Mexico: someone else has to hold it

The structural difference matters more than any individual labelling rule.

Per the US Commerce Department's
[country commercial guide](https://www.trade.gov/country-commercial-guides/mexico-labelingmarking-requirements),
labelling and broader NOM compliance **must be carried out by the Mexican importer or
another Mexican entity** holding an RFC — a Mexican tax ID.

You cannot be the compliance party from the US. That is not a paperwork detail; it decides
your route to market. Either you have a Mexican entity, or your importer or distributor
holds compliance and therefore holds a great deal of control over your product's presence in
the market.

**The standards themselves.** Products for retail sale must comply with Mexican Official
Standards (NOMs), in Spanish:

- **NOM-050** covers general commercial information and country-of-origin marking, and is
  the default where no product-specific NOM applies.
- **NOM-051** covers foods and non-alcoholic beverages, including front-of-pack labelling.

Compliance is verified at the border, and depending on the product, labelling may be
completed under customs control through authorised mechanisms — which is the practical
answer to goods that arrive unlabelled, at a cost.

⚠️ Sector-specific NOMs override the default, and their conformity assessment routes differ.
Check whether one applies to your product before assuming NOM-050. The Secretariat of
Economy's [SNICE portal](https://www.snice.gob.mx/cs/avi/snice/etiquetado.info.com.html) is
the official starting point.

## What actually transfers, and what doesn't

**Transfers well:** your test reports and supplier declarations. The underlying evidence
about what is in the product is reusable even where the legal frameworks are not.

**Transfers badly:** your certificates. A US
[GCC or CPC](/blog/gcc-vs-cpc-which-certificate-does-your.html) is an attestation against US
rules and means nothing in either country.

**Does not transfer at all:** your response processes. A two-day Canadian incident clock and
a Mexican compliance holder are operational arrangements, not documents, and they have to be
built before you need them.

## Before the first shipment

1. Decide who the Canadian importer is, and whether they or you hold the s.13 records.
2. Write the incident procedure to a **two-day** trigger, and name who decides.
3. Set retention to six years, and apply it to everything rather than by jurisdiction.
4. For Mexico, identify the entity holding NOM compliance and put it in the contract.
5. Check for a product-specific NOM before relying on NOM-050.
6. Decide where labelling happens — origin, or under customs control — and price it.
