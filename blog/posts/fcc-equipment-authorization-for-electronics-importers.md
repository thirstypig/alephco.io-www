CPSIA gets all the attention for consumer products, and FCC compliance gets skipped by
importers who don't think of themselves as "electronics companies" even though their
product has a Bluetooth chip in it. That gap is exactly what one FCC enforcement action
just cost a company over a million dollars to find out about.

Checked against the FCC's own procedures on **9 March 2027**.

## Two different processes, and most products need to know which

**Certification** applies to intentional radiators — anything that transmits, like a phone,
a wireless router, or a remote control. It's the more demanding path: review by an
FCC-recognized Telecommunication Certification Body and a formal FCC grant before the
product can be marketed, and certified devices are listed in the FCC's own public database.

**Supplier's Declaration of Conformity (SDoC)** applies to unintentional radiators — digital
devices that don't transmit but still generate RF energy internally, like a computer
peripheral, an LED bulb, or a switching power supply. This is a self-declaration: the party
responsible for compliance confirms the product meets the technical standard, tested but
without an FCC filing or database listing.

Many everyday products need both at once — a smartphone gets Certification for its radio
and separately has to meet SDoC standards for its non-radio digital circuitry.

## What CBP actually checks

There is no dedicated FCC import form anymore. **FCC Form 740 was eliminated in November
2017** because it duplicated data CBP already collects through its own entry system. What
that means in practice: the burden shifted to the importer being able to *produce*
equipment-authorization documentation on request, rather than filing it up front. If you
can't produce it when asked, "we didn't know we needed a form" isn't the gap — the
authorization itself is.

## CE marking doesn't substitute for any of this

There's no legal mechanism under which an EU CE mark satisfies US FCC requirements, and
vice versa. FCC authorization is granted against FCC technical standards, by FCC-recognized
bodies, under 47 CFR Part 2 — a framework that makes no reference anywhere to foreign
conformity marks or mutual recognition for this category of equipment. If your product is
CE-marked for the EU, that tells you nothing about its FCC status for the US.

## What this actually costs

**Sound Around, Inc.**, a Brooklyn-based electronics seller, received an FCC Notice of
Apparent Liability in December 2023 proposing a **$1,202,454** forfeiture for marketing 33
unauthorized RF devices and failing to fully cooperate with the FCC's investigation — its
second citation for the same conduct, after an earlier fine in 2022. The size of the
proposed penalty reflected the repeat-offense pattern and the company's demonstrated
ability to pay, not just the underlying violation.

Worth being precise here: a Notice of Apparent Liability is a *proposed* penalty, not yet a
final one — but the exposure is real either way, and repeat non-compliance is what turned
this into a seven-figure number.

## What this means practically

- **Classify every RF-emitting product before import, not after a complaint.** Intentional
  vs. unintentional radiator determines whether you need Certification, SDoC, or both.
- **CE marking is not a substitute for FCC authorization** — confirm both independently if
  you sell into both markets.
- **Be ready to produce equipment-authorization documentation on demand.** There's no form
  to file at the border anymore; there's only the question of whether you can answer it
  when asked.
