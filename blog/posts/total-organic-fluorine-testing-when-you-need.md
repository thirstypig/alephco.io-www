"Should we do TOF testing?" is the second question importers ask us about PFAS, right after
[which states require what](/blog/pfas-rules-for-importers.html). The answer is usually
"not yet, and not for the reason you think" — because a fluorine result does not tell you
whether you have a problem. It tells you who has to prove what.

Checked against the rules on **6 September 2026**.

## First, the term is wrong

The industry says *total organic fluorine* (TOF). The regulations mostly don't.

Washington and New Mexico both write **"total fluorine"** and **"fluorine"** — the broader
measurement, which counts inorganic fluorine too. Total organic fluorine is a narrower
number, and it is not the one those rules key on. If a lab quotes you TOF and a rule asks
about total fluorine, you have not answered the question that was asked.

This matters more than it sounds, because
[as covered previously](/blog/what-intentionally-added-pfas-actually-means.html), "PFAS" is
defined about as broadly as it can be — Minnesota's version is "a class of fluorinated
organic chemicals containing at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom" (§116.943 subd.
1(q)). Fluorine measurements are a proxy for that class, and proxies have edges.

## A fluorine result is a burden shift, not a limit

Here is the thing nobody says plainly: **none of these numbers are maximum permitted
levels.** They are the point at which the state stops having to prove intent and you start
having to disprove it.

Three trigger levels are in force, and they are not the same:

| Rule | Trigger | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| WAC 173-337, older categories | **Any detection** of total fluorine | Presumption of intentionally added PFAS, no floor at all |
| WAC 173-337, newer categories | Total fluorine **above 50 ppm** | Same presumption, with a floor |
| 20.13.2.14 NMAC (New Mexico) | Fluorine **above 100 ppm** | Same presumption, higher floor |

The Washington split is the one to notice, because summaries describe that state as having
"a 50 ppm total fluorine threshold" and that is only half true. The rule carries two
different sentences. For the newer product categories it reads:

> Ecology presumes the detection of total fluorine above 50 ppm indicates the intentional
> addition of PFAS.

For others, the same rule says simply:

> Ecology presumes the detection of total fluorine indicates the intentional addition of
> PFAS.

No threshold. Any detection. A result that comfortably clears 50 ppm in one category
triggers the presumption in another, under the same chapter.

## Rebutting is a filing, not an argument

Both states let you rebut, and both mean something specific by it. Washington requires a
statement submitted **to Ecology**, carrying the name and address of the person submitting
it, a statement that PFAS were not intentionally added, and **credible evidence** supporting
that statement. New Mexico puts it more briefly (20.13.2.14 NMAC): "A manufacturer must
rebut the presumption by demonstrating that the per- or poly-fluoroalkyl substances were not
intentionally added."

Either way, "we don't think it was added on purpose" is not a rebuttal. You need to be able
to say where the fluorine came from.

## So when do you actually need the test?

**Rarely, as a first step.** Testing is expensive per SKU, and it answers a question the
regulator hasn't asked you yet. Reporting forms want the CAS number, the function and a
concentration band — all of which come from your supplier, not a lab.

Testing earns its place when:

- **A supplier won't or can't answer**, and you need something to file. Note New Mexico
  requires documentation verifying analytical results where you report an exact figure.
- **You've been asked.** New Mexico's testing provision applies where there is reasonable
  suspicion *and* the product's reporting or labelling obligations were not met — so being
  tested is often a consequence of not having filed.
- **A supplier's answer and the product disagree** — a water-repellent finish with a "no
  PFAS" declaration is worth a check.
- **You're rebutting a presumption**, which is the case where you have no choice.

**Before you spend on a test:** get the declaration first. It is cheaper, it is what the
filing actually requires, and a test result without a supplier explanation still leaves you
unable to rebut anything.

## If you do test, ask for the right thing

- Specify **total fluorine** if you are sizing yourself against Washington or New Mexico —
  not TOF, unless you also want TOF.
- New Mexico's rule requires a "commercially available analytical method" run **unmodified**,
  by a lab certified by the department or a recognised national or regional certifying
  authority (20.13.2.7 NMAC B). An in-house test can qualify; a tweaked method does not.
- Record the **method, the reporting limit and the sample identity** alongside the number. A
  result you cannot tie to a specific SKU and method is not evidence.
- Test the **component**, not just the finished good. Both rules speak of a product *or
  product component*, and that is usually where the answer lives.

## The honest summary

Fluorine testing is a screening and rebuttal tool. It is not how you demonstrate
compliance, and it is not the first thing to buy. The first thing to buy is a straight
answer from your supplier, on the record, with a date on it — which is the cheap step
everybody skips on the way to the expensive one.
