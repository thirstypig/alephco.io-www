Most supplier declarations are collected to satisfy an internal checklist and are discovered,
at audit, to answer no question anybody actually asks. The fix is not a better template. It
is asking for what the rules require you to be able to say.

Checked on **6 September 2026**.

## Start from what you must file

If a rule requires you to report something, that is the shape of the answer you need. From
[Minn. Stat. §116.943 subd. 2(a)](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/116.943) and
[20.13.2.12 NMAC](https://www.env.nm.gov/opf/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2026/04/EXHIBIT-A-20.13.2-NMAC-FINAL.pdf),
a PFAS report needs a product identifier, the **purpose** the substance serves, the amount
**by CAS registry number** (exact or in a published band), and contact details.

So the declaration asks for those four things. Not "do you use any restricted substances?" —
see [getting a declaration out of a supplier](/blog/getting-a-pfas-declaration-out-of-an.html).

## What makes a declaration survive scrutiny

**A date.** A declaration is evidence about one moment. Undated, it cannot be aged, cannot be
re-checked, and cannot be shown to predate or postdate a change. This is the single most
common defect and the cheapest to fix.

**A named signatory with a role.** "Quality Department" is not a person. An auditor asking
"who told you that?" needs an answer.

**The specific product or component**, identified the way your records identify it. A
declaration covering "all products supplied" is worth very little, because nobody can show
which items were in scope on which date.

**The question, quoted.** Keep the question you asked alongside the answer. A "no" is only
meaningful against a definition — and as covered in
[what "intentionally added" means](/blog/what-intentionally-added-pfas-actually-means.html),
those definitions differ by state. A declaration recording only the answer has lost half the
evidence.

**A scope statement.** Does it cover the finished product, or the component the supplier
provides? Rules commonly reach a "product **or product component**", and a component-level
answer presented as a product-level one is a gap waiting to be found.

## What auditors actually probe

- **Currency.** Is it older than the interval it supports? For children's product testing
  those intervals are in [§1107.21](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/part-1107); for
  FSVP supplier approvals it is at least every three years under
  [§1.505(c)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/section-1.505).
- **Coverage.** Does it name this SKU, or a family it might not include?
- **Change.** What has happened since it was signed? §1107.23 makes a change in **component
  sourcing** a material change — and no system anywhere generates a notification.
- **Consistency.** Does the declaration agree with the test report and the certificate? Three
  documents saying slightly different things is worse than one.

## The question to ask every year

Not "has anything changed?", which reliably produces "no". Ask:

> **What changed since [date]?**

It is answerable, it is specific, and it invites the supplier to look rather than reassure.
The date in that question is the date on the last declaration — which only works if there is
one.

## Where they should live

Against the product record, with the date, the signatory, the question asked and the scope.
Not in an email thread, because retention periods outlast relationships — five years for
children's product records ([§1107.26](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/part-1107)),
two years for FSVP, and six years in Canada.

That is what Aleph does with declarations: the request goes out by link, the answer attaches
to the product with its date, and the follow-up question has somewhere to land.
