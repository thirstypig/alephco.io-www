PFAS Regulations for Importers: State Laws and Compliance

Your waterproof hiking jacket just failed a test you didn't know existed, for a chemical category most people can't pronounce. Welcome to the world of PFAS regulations — and it's moving faster than you think.

James Chang Written with AI
TL;DR
  • A growing list of US states have enacted PFAS restrictions on consumer products — with bans, reporting mandates, and labeling requirements that vary widely by state — and EPA’s TSCA rule adds a one-time federal PFAS report that covers importers
  • Product categories hit hardest include apparel, cookware, food packaging, children's products, carpets, and cosmetics
  • If you import treated or coated goods, you likely have disclosure obligations you don't know about yet
  • A centralized compliance tracking system beats the spreadsheet-and-prayer approach every time

What are PFAS and why should importers care?

PFAS stands for per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances. That's a mouthful, so most people just call them "forever chemicals." The name is earned: these synthetic compounds don't break down in the environment, not on any timescale that matters to a supply chain. They persist in water, soil, and the human body for years, which is exactly why regulators are starting to take notice.

PFAS are everywhere in consumer products because they're genuinely useful. They make things water-resistant, stain-resistant, and non-stick. Your rain jacket? Often treated with PFAS. Non-stick cookware? PFAS. Grease-proof takeout containers? PFAS. Stain-resistant carpet? You see where this is going.

Here's the part that matters for importers: the EPA has flagged PFAS as a major regulatory priority, and individual states have not waited for federal action. They're passing their own laws — at a pace that makes Prop 65 look slow by comparison. If you're bringing consumer goods into the US and you haven't checked your PFAS exposure, you're already behind.

Which states have PFAS laws affecting consumer products?

This is where it gets complicated. There's no broad federal ban on PFAS in consumer products. There is, however, a federal reporting obligation: EPA's TSCA PFAS reporting rule (40 CFR part 705) requires a one-time report from anyone who manufactured or imported PFAS — including PFAS in imported articles — at any time from 2011 through 2022. The submission period opens January 31, 2027, or earlier if EPA announces an earlier date. Bans and product-level rules, meanwhile, are a patchwork of state laws, each with different scopes, timelines, and requirements. Here's a summary of key states, checked against each state's statute in September 2026:

And that's not the complete list. Connecticut (reporting and labeling from July 1, 2026; sales ban from January 1, 2028) and New Mexico (first bans, reporting, and labeling from January 1, 2027) have also enacted broad laws, and other states have their own rules or pending bills. Maine and Minnesota go furthest — from January 1, 2032, both ban intentionally added PFAS in essentially all products sold in the state, unless the state has determined by rule that the use is currently unavoidable.

For importers, this means you can't just check one state's rules and call it a day. If you sell nationally, you need to track the most restrictive law that applies to each product category.

Product categories most affected by PFAS regulations

Not every product you import will have PFAS concerns. But if your catalog includes any of the following, you should be paying attention:

  • Textiles and apparel — water-resistant jackets, stain-resistant clothing, uniforms, outdoor gear. California's AB 1817 targets these directly.
  • Food packaging and food contact materials — grease-proof wrappers, microwave popcorn bags, paper plates, takeout containers. This is the category where the most states have already acted.
  • Cookware — non-stick pans, baking sheets, anything with a PTFE or similar coating.
  • Children's products — treated fabrics in car seats, strollers, bibs, and crib mattresses. If you're already navigating CPSIA and Children's Product Certificates, add PFAS to the checklist.
  • Carpets and rugs — stain-resistant treatments are a classic PFAS application.
  • Cosmetics and personal care — certain foundations, concealers, waterproof mascaras, and sunscreens.
  • Cleaning products — some floor waxes, polishes, and surface treatments contain PFAS.

If your product has been marketed as "waterproof," "stain-resistant," "grease-proof," or "non-stick," treat it as a PFAS suspect until proven otherwise.

How to determine if your products contain PFAS

This is the question that keeps importers up at night, because PFAS aren't always obvious. Your supplier might not even know whether their materials contain PFAS — especially if the treatment was applied further up the supply chain.

Here's a practical approach:

  1. Ask your suppliers directly. Request a written declaration stating whether the product or any of its components contain intentionally added PFAS. Be specific — ask about coatings, treatments, and packaging, not just the base material.
  2. Check Safety Data Sheets (SDS). If your products use chemical treatments, the SDS should list fluorinated compounds. Look for anything with "fluoro" in the chemical name.
  3. Request total organic fluorine (TOF) testing. This is a common screening method. Labs measure organic fluorine content, which serves as a proxy for PFAS presence. It won't tell you which PFAS compounds are present, or whether they were intentionally added, but it tells you where to look.
  4. Review your supply chain documentation. If you have an FSVP program or similar supplier verification process, extend it to include PFAS declarations.

The EPA's PFAS testing methods page provides technical guidance on validated analytical approaches if you need to go deeper.

PFAS disclosure and reporting requirements

Getting a test result is one thing. Knowing what you're required to do with that information is another. PFAS regulations generally fall into three buckets:

  • Outright bans — you simply cannot sell the product in that state if it contains intentionally added PFAS (and in some states, such as California, PFAS at or above 100 ppm total organic fluorine). California, New York, and Vermont all have food packaging bans in effect.
  • Disclosure and notification — you can still sell the product, but you must report PFAS content to a state agency. Minnesota (initial reports due September 15, 2026, then annual updates each February 1) and Connecticut (notification due July 1, 2026) fall here. Maine no longer has a general notification requirement; it now applies only to products granted a currently unavoidable use determination.
  • Labeling requirements — some states require on-product disclosure of PFAS content: Connecticut since July 1, 2026, and New Mexico from January 1, 2027. The idea is similar in concept to California's Prop 65 warnings.

The tricky part? These categories overlap and change. A state might start with disclosure, then phase in bans over a few years. Minnesota, for example, has a tiered timeline: certain product categories face restrictions starting in 2025, with a broader ban rolling out through 2032.

The biggest risk for importers isn't that PFAS regulations are complicated. It's that they're moving targets. What's compliant today might not be compliant in six months, and tracking that across a growing list of states manually is a recipe for missed deadlines.

Building a PFAS compliance program that scales

If you import more than a handful of SKUs, you need a system. Not a folder of PDFs and a hope that someone remembers to check when Minnesota's next phase kicks in. An actual system.

Here's what a solid PFAS compliance program looks like:

  1. Product inventory with PFAS flags. Every SKU in your catalog should have a field indicating whether it contains PFAS, is PFAS-free, or hasn't been tested yet. You can't manage what you haven't mapped.
  2. State-by-state regulatory mapping. For each product category, know which states have active restrictions and what those restrictions require. This matrix changes constantly — your system needs to handle updates.
  3. Supplier declarations on file. Collect and store PFAS declarations from every supplier. Set expiration dates and renewal reminders. A two-year-old declaration from a supplier who may have changed formulations is not compliance.
  4. Testing cadence. Establish a schedule for TOF screening, especially for high-risk categories. Annual testing is a reasonable starting point for most products.
  5. Disclosure tracking. If you sell into Minnesota or Connecticut, you need proof that you filed your report. If you sell food packaging in California, you need documentation that your products are below the 100 ppm total organic fluorine threshold. Track every filing, every deadline.

Can you do all of this in a spreadsheet? Sure, the same way you can technically drive cross-country in first gear. It's possible. It's just a terrible idea once you get past a few dozen products.

Key Takeaways
  1. PFAS regulations are a fast-moving patchwork — a growing list of states have laws on the books, with bans, reporting, and labeling requirements that vary by product category and state, plus a one-time federal TSCA report for manufacturers and importers
  2. If your products are marketed as water-resistant, stain-resistant, grease-proof, or non-stick, assume PFAS until proven otherwise
  3. Supplier declarations and TOF testing are your first line of defense — start collecting them now
